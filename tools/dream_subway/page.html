Dream Subway

Eight lines fitted to where one rider's bike actually goes. Every alignment follows a corridor the GPS has already worn in; every station sits on a place a ride has repeatedly started or ended. Nothing here is planned — it is measured.

The network

Drawn on the real street geometry, rotated so Manhattan's avenues stand upright and stretched east–west so the avenues separate. Behind the routes, in grey, is every street the rider has ridden more than once — the surface the lines were cut from.

Line guide

local and express patterns

A stop runs express if it is a transfer or if it is one of the fourteen places the rider most often starts or finishes — 26 arrivals or more over five years. The bar beside each segment is the measured traffic on it: average passes per stretch of street between those two stops.

Why these lines

Two measurements decide the map. Where rides end sets the stations; which streets carry the passes sets the alignments. Both come out lopsided, which is what makes a network possible at all: 2,814 trip ends fall into 329 places, and four of them take 44% of them.

What the network would reach

Of the 1,320 rides that ended somewhere other than where they began, four in five could be done end to end with an 800 m walk at each end. The 87 loop rides — out and back to the same door — are the ones no subway can serve, and the Park Loop is the answer to them.

How it was cut

  • Stations. The first and last fix of every ride CSV, clustered at a 300 m radius by density. A cluster becomes a stop where it lands on a corridor; the rest of the stops are the local maxima of pass traffic along the corridor, spaced at least 500–800 m apart. Names are the nearest Citi Bike dock, tidied.
  • Alignments. Streets ranked by pass-metres — length times the number of recorded crossings — out of the published rides.geojson. The top of that ranking is Broadway, Sixth Avenue, Second Avenue, the Hudson River Greenway and the two East River bridge paths, and those are the eight lines.
  • Express and local. Express stops are the transfers plus the fourteen busiest trip ends. Two lines run local only: the Park Loop, where every stop is a destination, and the Manhattan Bridge, which is too short for the distinction to buy anything.
  • Where it is thin. Second Avenue above E 63 St carries four to eight passes a stretch against ninety at Grand Street; the Crosstown east of First Avenue is the Queensboro path and little else. Both are drawn because the rides are there, not because the traffic justifies a train.
  • What it cannot say. Trip ends are where a recording started, which is not always where the rider did. Passes are counted per drawn segment, so a corridor of two parallel ways splits its traffic between them. And a bike is not a train: the map credits the greenway and the park drives with riding that is the point of the trip rather than a way through it.
